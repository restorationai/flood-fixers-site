## The 7 Signs That Point to Hidden Mold

Mold doesn't always show up as a fuzzy patch on the ceiling. Most of the time, it's growing somewhere you can't see, behind a wall, under flooring, inside a cabinet base, and the clues are subtler than people expect. Here's what to watch for:

1. **A musty smell that doesn't go away.** If a room smells like a damp basement even after you've aired it out, mold is likely producing that odor from inside a wall cavity or under the subfloor.
2. **Discoloration on walls or ceilings.** Yellowish, brown, or grayish staining, especially in a ring pattern, usually means water has been sitting against that material long enough for mold to take hold on the other side.
3. **Peeling paint or bubbling drywall.** Moisture trapped behind the surface pushes paint and paper away from the wall. If you press on the spot and it feels soft or spongy, there's likely saturation and mold behind it.
4. **Warped or cupped flooring.** Wood floors that lift at the seams, or laminate that feels uneven underfoot, often sit over a slab or subfloor that's held moisture for weeks.
5. **Unexplained allergy symptoms at home.** If you or someone in the house has a cough, sinus irritation, or watery eyes that clear up when you leave the house and come back when you're home, airborne mold spores are worth investigating.
6. **Condensation on windows or pipes that won't quit.** Persistent condensation means the air in that space is holding more moisture than it should, which is exactly the environment mold needs to spread.
7. **A past leak you thought you handled.** A slow slab leak, a roof leak after a storm, or a washing machine supply line that dripped for a few days, if it wasn't fully dried out, mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours and keep going long after the surface looks dry.

## Why This Shows Up So Often in San Diego Homes

A lot of San Diego's housing stock, especially homes built from the 1950s through the 1980s, sits on slab foundations with plumbing run through the slab itself. When a slab leak develops, water often travels sideways before it ever reaches the surface, soaking into framing and flooring along the way. Add in the marine layer that keeps coastal neighborhoods humid through the morning, and homes with older stucco exteriors or underinsulated attics can trap moisture longer than owners realize. None of that means every house has a mold problem, but it does mean a small leak here tends to have more time to turn into something bigger before anyone notices.

## What To Do If You Spot These Signs

- **Identify the moisture source first.** Mold needs ongoing moisture to keep growing, so look for the leak, the condensation point, or the humidity source before you touch anything cosmetic.
- **Check nearby areas, not just the obvious spot.** If you find staining on a ceiling, check the room above and the wall cavity below it too. Water moves along framing and insulation, not in a straight line down.
- **Take photos before you do anything else.** If you end up filing an insurance claim, dated photos of the stain, the smell source, or the damaged material help establish the timeline.
- **Improve airflow and reduce humidity where you can.** Running a dehumidifier or opening windows on dry days can slow mold growth while you figure out next steps, though it won't remove an existing colony.
- **Avoid sealing the area up tighter.** It's tempting to shut a door on a musty room, but stagnant air with no circulation gives mold exactly the stable environment it needs.

## What Not To Do

Don't paint over a stain and call it fixed. Paint can mask discoloration for a few months, but it doesn't address what's happening underneath, and mold will usually bleed through again. Don't rip into drywall or flooring yourself if you suspect a larger colony. Disturbing mold without containment can release spores into the air and spread the problem to rooms that were previously clean. And don't rely on bleach as a long-term fix. Bleach can lighten surface staining on non-porous materials, but it doesn't kill mold growing inside porous materials like drywall paper or wood, so the problem often returns within weeks.

## When It's Time to Call a Professional

A small, isolated spot you can see and reach, say, a few inches of mold on a bathroom ceiling tile from a known leak that's already fixed, is often something a homeowner can clean up themselves with the right precautions. But if any of these apply, it's worth getting a professional opinion before you do more work:

- The affected area is larger than about 10 square feet
- You can smell mold but can't find where it's coming from
- The source was a significant water event: a burst pipe, a roof leak, flooding, or a slab leak
- Someone in the home has ongoing respiratory symptoms
- You're preparing to sell the home and need documentation of the condition

A mold inspection identifies not just where the visible growth is, but where moisture has traveled that you can't see from the surface. Remediation work typically follows guidelines like the IICRC S520 standard, which covers containment, air filtration, and removal of affected materials, so spores aren't spread to unaffected parts of the home during the cleanup.

## The Recovery Process, Step by Step

Once a moisture source is confirmed and contained, remediation generally moves through a few stages: containment of the work area to stop spores from traveling, removal of mold-affected materials that can't be cleaned (often sections of drywall, insulation, or flooring), HEPA-filtered air scrubbing to pull spores out of the air, and drying the structure down to a safe moisture level before any rebuild begins. Depending on what was removed, reconstruction, new drywall, flooring, or trim, may follow once the area tests clean and dry. The timeline varies a lot depending on how far moisture traveled and what materials were affected, so it's hard to put a single number on it without seeing the space.
