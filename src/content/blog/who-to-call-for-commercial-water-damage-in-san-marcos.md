The first call is a commercial water damage restoration company, because extraction and drying have to start within hours to limit the loss. ## Should You Call a Plumber, 911, or the Restoration Company First?

Call whoever stops the hazard fastest: a plumber if water is still actively flowing from a fixture you can't isolate, 911 or the utility company if there's an electrical or gas hazard, and the restoration company for everything else, including extraction itself. These aren't competing calls, they're sequential.

If you can safely reach the shutoff valve for the fixture or line that failed, do that first; it's often faster than waiting for a plumber to arrive just to turn a valve. If the water is near an electrical panel, outlets, or any equipment that's still powered, don't wade in. Cut power at the breaker if you can do it without stepping in water, and call the utility or an electrician if you can't.

Once the source is stopped or isolated, the restoration company becomes the priority call, because every additional hour of standing water adds cost and risk. A sewer or drain backup from a floor drain is a different situation entirely: that water is Category 3 (grossly contaminated) and needs a crew in appropriate PPE, not a mop and a shop vac. Never treat a backup like a simple leak.

## What Happens When You Call a Commercial Water Damage Company?

The first call establishes what flooded, how much square footage is affected, and whether any part of the building needs to be evacuated or powered down before a crew arrives. Have your suite or unit number, the approximate source of the water, and your property manager's contact information ready.

On arrival, the crew typically does a walk-through with a moisture meter and thermal imaging to map how far water traveled under flooring and behind walls, since commercial water damage often spreads farther than it looks from the surface. They'll set extraction equipment first, then begin [structural drying](/service-areas/san-marcos-ca/water-damage-restoration/) using air movers and dehumidifiers sized to the space, following the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for professional water damage restoration.

For a business, the useful question is whether drying can be phased so part of the building stays open. Often yes: a crew can contain the affected zone with plastic sheeting and isolate the drying equipment to one wing or floor while the rest of the space continues operating, especially in multi-tenant buildings where only one suite took on water. Ask about this directly; it's one of the biggest differences between a residential and a commercial scope of work.

## What Does a Commercial Property Insurance Adjuster Need to See?

A commercial adjuster needs photo and moisture documentation from before any materials are removed, a clear timeline of when the loss was discovered and when mitigation started, and a written scope of the drying work performed. Commercial property policies generally require the business to show reasonable steps were taken to limit the damage, so documentation protects the claim as much as it protects the building.

Ask the restoration crew to log moisture readings daily and photograph the affected area before, during, and after drying. Standard commercial property policies typically cover sudden, accidental water damage, such as a burst supply line or a failed rooftop unit, but usually exclude gradual leaks and flood water from outside the building unless a separate flood policy applies, per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance). Review your specific commercial policy language, since business interruption and equipment breakdown endorsements vary by carrier.
