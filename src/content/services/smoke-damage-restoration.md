Smoke doesn't need direct flame to damage a home. During Santa Ana wind events, wildfire smoke drifts into attics, HVAC returns, and wall cavities of homes miles from any fire line, leaving behind an acidic film that keeps etching glass, metal, and painted surfaces for days after the air clears. Inside a house with an actual fire, the soot layer forms within minutes and starts bonding to porous materials as it cools. Either way, the longer residue sits, the more it migrates from a surface cleaning problem into a replacement problem.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is the identification and removal of soot residue, the neutralization of the odor-causing particles trapped in porous materials, and the treatment of HVAC systems that would otherwise keep recirculating smoke smell for months. It's distinct from fire damage reconstruction: smoke work focuses on residue and air quality rather than rebuilding burned structure. On a wildfire infiltration case, most of the job is odor and fine particulate, since there's no direct heat damage to materials. On a structure fire, smoke cleanup runs alongside soot removal from framing, ductwork, and contents. Equipment typically includes HEPA air scrubbers, thermal foggers, ozone or hydroxyl generators for odor-causing molecules, and chemical sponges formulated for dry versus wet soot. Timelines range from a single day for light odor cases to a week or more when ductwork and whole-house contents need processing.

## Our process

1. **Soot and residue characterization.** We identify whether the residue is dry soot from a fast, high-heat fire, wet/oily soot from a smoldering or synthetic-material fire, or fine wildfire particulate, because the cleaning agents and tools differ for each.
2. **HVAC and duct assessment.** Smoke particles pulled through a running system settle in ductwork and get redistributed every time the blower cycles. We inspect the system and clean or seal it before doing surface work, otherwise odor returns within days.
3. **Surface and content cleaning.** Dry sponging for dry soot, solvent-based cleaning for oily residue, and specialized treatment for electronics, upholstery, and porous contents that absorbed smoke before they were removed from the space.
4. **Odor treatment.** Once surfaces are clean, we treat remaining odor-causing molecules with ozone or hydroxyl generators and thermal fogging, matched to the material and occupancy situation, since ozone requires the space to be vacated during treatment.
5. **Sealing and documentation.** Affected drywall, framing, or trim that can't be fully deodorized gets sealed with an odor-blocking primer before paint, and we document pre- and post-treatment conditions for the insurance file.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is cleaning visible surfaces while skipping the HVAC system, which means the smell comes back the first time the heat or AC runs. Another is sealing and painting over residue before odor treatment is complete, which traps the smell instead of removing it. Acidic soot left untreated on metal fixtures, mirrors, and glass continues etching the surface for days, so timing matters more than people expect. Insurance adjusters generally want to see documentation distinguishing pre-existing conditions from smoke-related damage, room-by-room residue photos, and a clear scope separating cleaning from any necessary replacement, which is why we document conditions before starting work rather than after.
